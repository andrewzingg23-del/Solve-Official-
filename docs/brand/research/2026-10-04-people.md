# Consumer research: what people feel, and who to serve first

Date: 2026-10-04. Part of the brand v2 research (`docs/brand/BRAND.md`).

**How this was sourced.** A research agent searched the web. Direct page fetches were blocked, so the figures come from search-indexed text of the primary source or from press coverage of it. **[unverified]** marks a claim that couldn't be tied to a primary source. Spot-check any figure before quoting it outside the company.

## Use is high, trust is low, and control is wanted

- **Trust lags use.** Across 47 countries, 66% use AI regularly but only 46% are willing to trust it [1]. In the US, 61% have used AI and only 3% pay for it [5].
- **Control is wanted.** 50% of Americans are more concerned than excited (37% in 2021), and 61% want more control over how AI is used in their lives [2].
- **Gen Z is souring.** In 2026, weekly use held at 51%, but excitement fell 14 points to 22% and anger rose to 31% [4]. 61% of adults under 30 expect AI to make people worse at creative thinking [2].
- **Millennial parents are the power users.** Daily use: parents 29% vs non-parents 15% [5].
- **Money is the line people won't cross:**
  - 64% would let AI manage calendars; 39% would trust it with financial planning [11].
  - Only 24% trust agents to make routine purchases [12].

## Saying "AI" costs trust; showing the work earns it

- **The word lowers purchase intent.** Adding "artificial intelligence" to identical product descriptions lowered trust and purchase intent, and more so for high-risk purchases [15].
- **Disclosure lowers trust.** Across 13 experiments, people who disclosed AI use were trusted less [16].
- **AI ads hurt the brand.** They were rated more annoying, boring and confusing, and the dislike spilled onto the brand [18].
- **Content preference.** 50% of US consumers prefer brands that avoid generative AI in consumer-facing content, and 68% often wonder whether content is real [21].
- **"Slop"** was Merriam-Webster's 2025 word of the year [23].
- **Visible process and effort raise perceived value** [26][28].

## Agency, ownership and the fear of getting worse

- **Effort and control create ownership:**
  - People value AI co-creations more when they put in more of their own effort [27].
  - People accept imperfect algorithms when they can adjust the output [31].
  - Control over the AI increases ownership of the result [30].
- **People resist automation that takes credit for what they identify with** [32].
- **Deskilling is a real cost:**
  - Intrinsic motivation fell 11% when people went back to working without AI [29].
  - Students with unrestricted GPT did 17% worse once it was removed, while a tutor version with guardrails avoided this [33].
- **AI users are judged lazier and less competent,** unless the task is clearly suited to AI [17].

## Life and economic context

- **Independent work:** 72.9M US independent workers, 27.6M of them full time [35]. 28% of skilled knowledge workers work independently [36].
- **Business formation:** a record 5.67M US business applications in 2025, against 3.50M in 2019 [37]. Businesses with no employees grow faster than employers [38].
- **Side hustles are cooling and small:** 27% of adults have one (36% in 2024), with a median income of $200 a month [40].
- **Gen Z founders use AI:** they were 9% of new business starts in 2025, and 71% used AI to launch (Gusto's own data) [41].
- **Makers:** entry-level 3D printer shipments rose 26% in 2025 and 47% in Q4, and Bambu Lab holds a 37% share [43].
- **Strain:**
  - 73% of adults are doing at least okay financially, down from 78% [45].
  - 54% feel isolated [46].
  - Global employee engagement is 21% [48].

## Language

- **Avoid "AI" and "AI-powered" in brand messaging** [15][22].
- **"Agent" confuses consumers,** who sometimes link it to customer support, and they trust help with tasks more than AI acting on its own [12].
- **Avoid "automation" and "does it for you",** which run into identity [32] and the laziness penalty [17].
- **Gap:** no controlled test compares "agent", "assistant" or "bot". Test before committing.

## Audiences to serve first

| Audience | Job to be done | Frustration | Proof they need | Lead with |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Makers with physical-object ideas** (3D-printer owners, crafters) | Turn a design into something made and sold | Sourcing; marketplaces policing AI work (Etsy separates "made by" from "designed by" [49]) | Physical prototypes, supplier quotes, a record showing the design is theirs | Craft and authorship |
| **Gen Z and millennial side-builders** | Get an idea to first revenue without capital or quitting | Too many tools, tight cash, fear of looking like an AI faker | A real cost and time ledger, first sales, spend only with approval | Authorship, honest money |
| **Time-poor millennial parents** | Finish the project they keep putting off, in stolen hours | No time; fear of runaway spending | Visible spending caps, approval gates, hours saved | Time and control |
| **Mid-career experts going solo** | Turn expertise into a one-person business | Burnout, admin | A checkable track record of *their* judgment | Track record |

## Rules this research implies

1. The person is the hero and the outcome is the headline. Never lead with "AI-powered".
2. Recast "stay in your seat" as the driver's seat, not the couch.
3. Make the "yes" visible: approvals, spending caps and undo in every demo.
4. Show the work. The proof record is the brand.
5. Disclose specifically: what the AI did and what the person decided.
6. Promise capability, not replacement. Show people getting better.
7. Use only real evidence and label anything simulated. Don't use AI-generated imagery in brand ads.
8. Be honest about money: modest first wins, transparent costs, no hustle hype.
9. Show humans: suppliers, collaborators, community.
10. Tune by generation: craft and authorship for Gen Z, time and control for parents.
